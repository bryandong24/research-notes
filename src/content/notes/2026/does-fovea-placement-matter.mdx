Before learning a gaze policy, we need to establish a more basic fact: **does the location of
preserved visual detail affect control at all?**

We tested this with a released [Unified Video and Action
Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00200) (UVA) policy on PushT. The policy was frozen. We changed
only its image observation, held the nominal source-resolution allocation fixed, and moved a
high-resolution region toward or away from the object being pushed.

The result is a strong placement dose-response. With the high-resolution region centered on the
T-block, the policy completed 62% of 50 rollouts. Moving the same region by half a foveal radius
reduced success to 40%; at one radius it fell to 8%. But this is not a claim that foveation
outperforms full-resolution vision, and it is not yet a speed result. It is a controlled
mechanism assay showing that **which pixels retain detail can materially alter a frozen
policy's behavior**.

## The intervention

PushT provides an RGB observation $I\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times96\times96}$. We first build a coarse
global image by downsampling to $12\times12$ and restoring the original raster dimensions:

$$
P = U_{96}\left(D_{12}(I)\right).
$$

Given a binary high-resolution mask $M$, the policy receives

$$
I_{\mathrm{fov}}
=
M\odot I+(1-M)\odot P.
$$

Pixels inside $M$ retain their source values. Pixels outside it come from the upsampled global
image. The resulting observation is still an ordinary dense $96\times96$ RGB tensor, so the
policy's architecture and compute are unchanged.

This distinction matters. The experiment tests **spatial information allocation and
frozen-model compatibility**. It does not use a variable-length token sequence, reduce UVA's
FLOPs, or require a new projection or decoder.

### Matching the nominal source-resolution budget

The foveated condition combines a $12\times12$ global read with exactly 432 high-resolution
source pixels:

$$
B_{\mathrm{fov}}
=12^2+432
=576.
$$

The uniform comparison downsamples the entire image to $24\times24$:

$$
B_{\mathrm{uniform}}
=24^2
=576.
$$

This bookkeeping assigns both conditions a nominal budget of 576 source-resolution values, or
6.25% of the 9,216-pixel raster. The foveated mask always contains exactly 432 restored source
pixels, including near image boundaries.

This is a **nominal resolution/information budget**, not a trace of an acquisition process. The
$12\times12$ and $24\times24$ images are formed by area interpolation from the complete
$96\times96$ simulator frame, so each coarse value averages an image region rather than reading
one independently chosen source pixel. The dense tensor ultimately contains 9,216 displayed
pixels in either case, and UVA processes all of them. Accordingly, “6.25%” does not establish a
camera-bandwidth, codec-bit-rate, entropy, or FLOP reduction.

## The oracle selector

PushT renders the manipulated T-block with a known color. A privileged selector identifies the
block in the current simulator frame and places the fovea at its centroid. The selector can see
the full source frame, but the frozen policy receives only $I_{\mathrm{fov}}$.

This is deliberately an upper-bound intervention, not a deployable gaze policy. It lets us vary
placement quality without simultaneously changing selector training.

We measure realized placement with object recall:

$$
q
=
\frac{|M\cap T|}{|T|},
$$

where $T$ is the set of pixels belonging to the current T-block. To construct a dose-response,
we displace the oracle center by

$$
\frac{d}{r}\in\{0,\;0.5,\;1,\;1.5,\;2\},
$$

where $r$ is the effective foveal radius. Displacement directions are balanced across the four
cardinal directions.

## What “reward” and “success” mean

PushT evaluates geometric overlap between the block and the goal. At timestep $t$, coverage is

$$
c_t
=
\frac{\operatorname{area}(T_t\cap G)}
       {\operatorname{area}(G)}.
$$

The environment maps this to

$$
r_t
=
\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{c_t}{0.95},0,1\right).
$$

The continuous score reported below is the maximum reward reached during the episode:

$$
R=\max_t r_t.
$$

An episode counts as an exact success when $R=1$, corresponding to the environment's 95%
coverage threshold. Here “exact success” means crossing that discrete simulator completion
criterion; it does not mean literal 100% geometric overlap. The underlying score is a
simulator-defined geometric reward—not a learned reward model or a human rating.

These two metrics expose different failure modes. A policy can move the block close to the goal
and receive a high maximum reward without ever crossing the exact-completion threshold.

## Experiment sequence

The study proceeded in three stages.

### 1. Restoration and deletion gate

A small paired screen first tested whether the frozen policy responded to spatially localized
information at all. It compared full resolution, uniform downsampling, object-centered
restoration, restoration displaced by two radii, deleting the object region from a
full-resolution frame, and deleting an equal-area background region.

In that exploratory screen:

- object-centered restoration exceeded the two-radius condition by 0.315 mean reward;
- deleting the task region hurt 0.118 more than deleting background;
- full resolution exceeded uniform sampling by only 0.016.

These numbers served only as a gate to the larger paired study.

We also ran one predeclared tighter-budget stress test:

$$
8^2+192=256=16^2,
$$

or a nominal 2.78% source-resolution budget. At this smaller allocation, object-centered foveation
was 0.168 reward below uniform sampling, although it remained 0.318 above a two-radius fovea.
Concentrating an extremely small budget into one region can therefore discard too much global
information.

### 2. Placement curve

The main placement assay evaluated 50 paired rollouts per condition, each with a 300-step
horizon. It included full and uniformly coarse references, peripheral-only input, geometric
center, a fixed random location, and five controlled object-relative offsets.

| Visual condition | Mean episode-max reward | Exact success | Object recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Full resolution | 0.972 | 68% | — |
| Uniform $24\times24$ | 0.903 | 20% | — |
| Object-centered, 0 radii | 0.894 | 62% | 0.738 |
| Displaced 0.5 radii | 0.847 | 40% | 0.640 |
| Displaced 1 radius | 0.679 | 8% | 0.402 |
| Displaced 1.5 radii | 0.594 | 4% | 0.199 |
| Displaced 2 radii | 0.580 | 6% | 0.079 |
| Center fovea | 0.772 | 26% | 0.376 |
| Fixed random fovea | 0.668 | 8% | 0.061 |
| $12\times12$ periphery only | 0.719 | 4% | — |

The paired episode-level slope was

$$
\frac{\Delta R}{\Delta(d/r)}
=-0.176,
\qquad
95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[-0.227,-0.125],
$$

while the success-probability slope was

$$
\frac{\Delta P(\mathrm{success})}{\Delta(d/r)}
=-0.296,
\qquad
95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[-0.360,-0.228].
$$

The overall decline is clear. The data do not establish a high-quality placement plateau: the
apparent flattening between 1.5 and 2 radii is a poor-performance floor.

### 3. Fixed nominal-budget region allocation

The placement curve shows that object coverage matters, but it does not tell us whether the
object should receive the entire budget. The next ablation keeps the same 432 high-resolution
pixels and compares:

- 432 pixels around the current object;
- 432 pixels around the fixed goal;
- 216 object pixels plus 216 goal pixels;
- 216 object pixels plus 216 matched-decoy pixels.

The decoy lies at the same object-relative distance as the goal but is rotated by
$\pm90^\circ$. It controls for the generic effect of introducing a second spatial lobe or mask
boundary. If the two regions overlap, the renderer refills their quotas with the nearest unused
pixels, keeping the union at exactly 432.

PushT uses a fixed goal at image center, so the earlier center condition and goal-only condition
are the same realized intervention.

| Allocation | Mean episode-max reward | Exact success | Object recall | Visible-goal recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Full resolution | 0.972 | 68% | — | — |
| Uniform $24\times24$ | 0.903 | 20% | — | — |
| Object, 432 pixels | 0.894 | 62% | 0.738 | 0.419 |
| Goal, 432 pixels | 0.772 | 26% | 0.376 | 0.704 |
| Object 216 + goal 216 | 0.918 | 46% | 0.568 | 0.628 |
| Object 216 + decoy 216 | 0.883 | 34% | 0.561 | 0.261 |

The split allocation has the highest reduced-input reward point estimate, but lower exact
success than object-only allocation. Moving half of the high-resolution pixels away from the
object reduces object recall from 0.738 to 0.568.

A predeclared tradeoff screen required the object+goal allocation to satisfy all three of the
following:

1. reward noninferiority to uniform sampling within 0.03;
2. exact-success superiority to uniform sampling;
3. exact-success noninferiority to object-only allocation within 0.10.

Only the second criterion passed. The 50/50 split is therefore not a free improvement.

## What the controls isolate

- **Full resolution** is the in-distribution reference.
- **Nominally equal-budget uniform sampling** tests concentrated versus spatially distributed
  detail under the same reconstruction procedure.
- **Peripheral-only input** measures what the coarse global stream can support by itself.
- **Center/goal placement** supplies a simple geometric baseline.
- **Random placement** tests an arbitrary fovea under the same nominal allocation.
- **Controlled displacement** gives a dose-response rather than one favorable oracle contrast.
- **Object versus background deletion** checks whether task-local information matters and
  exposes possible boundary cues.
- **The matched decoy** separates goal-specific information from generic two-region coverage.
- **Exact high-resolution pixel quotas** prevent edge location or region overlap from silently
  changing the restored-pixel allocation.
- **Paired rollouts with shared action-sampling randomness** reduce variance in condition
  differences.

## Interpreting reward and completion

Object-centered foveation and uniform sampling have nearly identical mean maximum rewards:
0.894 versus 0.903. Their exact success rates are very different: 62% versus 20%.

The paired reward difference is

$$
-0.009,\qquad95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[-0.068,0.042].
$$

No mean-reward advantage was detected, and this interval does not establish equivalence. The
paired exact-success difference is

$$
+0.42,\qquad95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[0.26,0.58].
$$

A careful reading is that uniform sampling often preserves enough distributed information to
approach the target, while object-centered detail changes how often the controller crosses the
final completion threshold.

The allocation study adds a second lesson. Object+goal has a slightly higher reward point
estimate than object-only, but exact success falls from 62% to 46%. This is consistent with
budget dilution, not proof that global planning improved. Likewise, the object+goal comparison
with object+decoy is inconclusive; its intervals cross zero. The study establishes neither that
goal detail is irrelevant nor that it provides a goal-specific benefit.

## What this study establishes

For this frozen UVA policy and PushT setting:

1. the unadapted policy extracts useful information from a dense foveated reconstruction;
2. placement quality has a strong graded relationship with closed-loop performance;
3. manipulated-object detail is the dominant requirement for exact completion at this nominal
   allocation;
4. foveation is a **budget-allocation problem**, not merely a gaze-coordinate problem;
5. adding a second region can hurt when it makes the primary region too coarse.

## What it does not establish

- **No speedup was measured.** UVA still processes a dense image through its unchanged visual
  stack.
- **This is not true mixed-resolution-token inference.** The intervention reconstructs a
  same-shaped raster.
- **The policy was not adapted to foveated observations.** These are frozen-model sensitivity
  results, not achievable performance after matched training.
- **The oracle is post-acquisition and privileged.** It examines the current full-resolution
  frame and does not reduce camera readout.
- **The oracle is not human gaze.** It is a simulator-derived object selector.
- **Foveated performance was not shown equivalent to full resolution.** Full resolution reached
  68% success versus 62% object-centered, and the uncertainty still permits a meaningful
  difference. Full resolution also had higher mean maximum reward.
- **The object centroid was not proven globally optimal.** It is one useful upper-bound
  location.
- **A moving mask can carry a location cue.** Not every effect can be attributed exclusively to
  recovered texture.
- **PushT has a fixed centered goal.** It is a weak task for isolating the value of
  goal-specific detail.
- **The result is task- and model-specific.** It does not establish transfer to physical
  sensing, other robot tasks, or other world-action architectures.
- **Action-sampling replication remains limited.** The paired assay controls one common
  sampling realization; broader replication is needed for a definitive policy-level estimate.
- **Object and goal recall are diagnostics.** Vectorized evaluation can repeat terminal
  observations, so these averages are not unbiased per-decision measurements.

## Why this matters for the gaze study

The PushT study answers the prerequisite question: **selector quality has enough leverage to
change behavior**. It does not tell us how a practical selector should find the useful region.

The next study therefore moves from a privileged object oracle to source-consistent learned
gaze on GIAVA thread needle. There, each gaze source trains its own complete downstream policy.
That experiment asks whether task-domain predicted gaze, generic visual saliency, fixed spatial
priors, and uniformly coarse vision remain distinguishable after the policy has adapted to each
observation distribution.

Together, the two studies separate mechanism from implementation:

1. **PushT:** under a fixed nominal source-resolution allocation, where detail is preserved
   affects control.
2. **GIAVA:** practical gaze sources differ after matched policy training.
3. **Next:** use action-conditioned relevance from a capable VLA or world-action model to
   supervise a small causal selector.

The resulting selector should observe only information available before acquisition—low-resolution
context, language, proprioception, and history—and allocate high-resolution sensing for a lighter
downstream policy. Only then can we test genuine token, latency, or sensor-bandwidth savings.
