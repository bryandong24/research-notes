The current experiments establish that high-resolution placement matters and that a
task-domain gaze predictor can outperform generic image saliency. They do not solve the
general case.

For a new task, collecting human eye tracking and training a task-specific gaze model each time
is expensive. A generic saliency model is easy to deploy, but it answers “what attracts human
fixation in an image?” rather than “where would additional spatial resolution change this
robot's next action?”

The proposed bridge is knowledge distillation:

```text
large full-resolution VLA or WAM, used offline
→ action-conditioned visual relevance
→ counterfactual resolution-utility labels
→ small causal foveation policy
→ lightweight matched-source VLA or WAM
```

The large teacher is deliberately absent from the efficient runtime path. Its role is to provide
privileged supervision that would be too expensive—or causally impossible—to compute during
deployment.

## What the existing evidence changes

Two observations motivate the program.

First, a fixed-budget PushT assay showed that preserving detail around the manipulated object
was substantially more useful than uniformly distributing the same source-image resolution.
Second, in thread needle, a policy trained and evaluated with task-domain predicted gaze
reached 86% exact success, versus 70% with DeepGaze-MIT and 53.3% with uniformly low-resolution
tokens.

The narrow conclusion is:

> A selector is part of the control system. It matters whether the selector follows
> action-relevant task geometry rather than generic visual saliency.

Several stronger claims remain open. The present task-specific predictor is not
language-conditioned, its gaze coordinate is also given numerically to the policy, and the
experiments are post-acquisition rather than true active sensing. Human-like gaze is therefore
a strong reference, not assumed robot-optimal ground truth.

## Synthetic task utility without hand-coded object masks

Synthetic utility should not mean manually labeling a “hand,” “needle,” “hole,” or “object” and
declaring it important. Those labels help interpret a result, but they should not define the
selector.

Instead, start with a semantically neutral grid or continuous spatial sampler. Let downstream
action behavior determine where higher resolution has value.

Let:

- $x^{HR}$ be a full-resolution observation;
- $x^{LR}$ be its low-resolution counterpart;
- $F(x^{HR},x^{LR},m)$ restore region $m$ at high resolution;
- $\pi_T$ be a competent teacher evaluated on its native full-resolution input;
- $\pi_R$ be a mask-robust evaluator or student trained to consume randomized
  low-/mixed-resolution allocations;
- $q$ be robot state and $l$ be the instruction.

The distinction between $\pi_T$ and $\pi_R$ is essential. The full-resolution teacher supplies
the target action distribution,

$$
y_T=\pi_T(x^{HR},q,l),
$$

while the mask-robust evaluator estimates how much of that target survives a candidate
allocation. Before labeling masks, train $\pi_R$ on randomized allocations:

$$
\mathcal L_R
=
\mathbb E_{m\sim p_{\mathrm{rand}}}
\left[
D\left(
\pi_R(F(x^{HR},x^{LR},m),q,l),
y_T
\right)
\right].
$$

The randomization should span position, scale, number of regions, and the all-low-resolution
case. Held-out mask patterns and demonstration actions then test whether $\pi_R$ is genuinely
allocation-robust rather than merely adapted to one renderer.

### Mask-robust teacher-consistency utility

For a candidate mask $m$:

$$
U_{\mathrm{teacher}}(m)
=
-D\left(
\pi_R(F(x^{HR},x^{LR},m),q,l),
y_T
\right).
$$

$D$ can compare action distributions, flow fields, or sampled action trajectories. The useful
mask is the one that best preserves what the teacher would do with full-resolution input.

A cheaper direct-perturbation screen replaces $\pi_R$ with $\pi_T$ and evaluates the
full-resolution teacher on foveated inputs. That shortcut is only preliminary unless the
teacher has independently demonstrated robustness to those allocations: otherwise the score
mixes information loss with an out-of-distribution response to the renderer.

### Demonstration-action utility

When a demonstration action $a^*$ is available:

$$
U_{\mathrm{BC}}(m)
=
-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{action}}\left(
\pi_R(F(x^{HR},x^{LR},m),q,l),
a^*
\right).
$$

This asks which allocation most supports the demonstrated behavior. It is useful when the
teacher's full-resolution action is not the only acceptable trajectory, but it also inherits
demonstration noise and multimodality.

### Resolution-upgrade value

Ordinary saliency identifies important regions in a full-resolution image. Foveation needs a
more specific quantity: **where does changing low resolution into high resolution add useful
information?**

$$
\Delta U(m)
=
D\left(
\pi_R(x^{LR},q,l),
y_T
\right)
-
D\left(
\pi_R(F(x^{HR},x^{LR},m),q,l),
y_T
\right).
$$

A large object can be semantically important yet remain recognizable when coarse. It may have
high saliency and low $\Delta U$. A small contact boundary can have modest global saliency but
large resolution-upgrade value.

That distinction is the central target of this proposal.

### Soft and multi-region targets

Many frames have more than one useful allocation. Convert utilities to a distribution:

$$
p_T(m)
=
\operatorname{softmax}\left(U(m)/\tau\right).
$$

For multiple foveae, estimate conditional marginal value:

$$
\Delta U(m_j\mid m_1,\ldots,m_{j-1}).
$$

This permits object-target pairs, two hands, or exploratory coverage without hard-coding
semantic categories.

## Candidate signals from a full-resolution teacher

A capable VLA or WAM exposes several possible relevance signals:

1. action-query attention to visual tokens;
2. [attention rollout](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00928) across residual layers;
3. gradient-weighted attention;
4. gradients of action outputs or action loss with respect to visual tokens;
5. patch deletion sensitivity;
6. low-to-high-resolution restoration value;
7. action-distribution entropy reduction;
8. ensemble or world-model uncertainty reduction;
9. attention to predicted future visual state.

For a flow-matching action decoder with vector field $v_\theta$, one gradient map is:

$$
R_i
=
\mathbb E_{\tau,\epsilon}
\left\|
\frac{\partial v_\theta(a_\tau,\tau,c)}
     {\partial h_i^{\mathrm{visual}}}
\right\|.
$$

The expectation over flow times and action noise avoids interpreting one arbitrary denoising
state.

Different WAM families expose different causal paths:

| Teacher family | Relevance path to inspect |
|---|---|
| VLA with action decoder | Action queries or action loss to visual-language hidden states |
| IDM-style WAM | Inverse-dynamics output to predicted future visual features |
| Single-backbone WAM | Action output channels to shared current/future visual tokens |
| MoT WAM | Action expert to current-video and future-video expert states |
| Spatial latent WAM | Action loss or uncertainty to grounded latent tokens |

## Attention is a hypothesis, not a label

Raw attention is appealing because it is available in one forward pass. It is not guaranteed to
measure causal importance; [attention weights can fail to provide faithful explanations of a
model's prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10186).

- Residual paths and MLPs can bypass an attention edge.
- Heads and layers specialize differently.
- High attention does not imply that high spatial resolution is necessary.
- Attention can highlight the robot itself even when proprioception already represents its
  configuration.
- A visually subtle contact boundary can have low attention but high action sensitivity.

Every attention-derived map must therefore pass a counterfactual test:

> Under the same visual budget, does preserving its top-ranked region retain action behavior
> better than preserving center, random, shuffled, low-ranked, stationary-prior, or generic
> saliency regions?

Agreement with recorded human gaze is valuable for interpretation. It is not sufficient for
promotion. A map that disagrees with human fixation but preserves control more effectively may
be the better robot sensing target.

## The deployable selector

The selector should operate before current high-resolution measurements are available:

$$
\hat G_{t+\Delta}
=
f_\phi(
x^{LR}_{\leq t},
q_{\leq t},
l,
a_{<t},
r^{HR}_{<t}
).
$$

Inputs include:

- current and recent low-resolution global observations;
- instruction;
- proprioception;
- earlier actions;
- past high-resolution crops and their locations.

Outputs can include:

- a probability distribution over spatial patches;
- top-$K$ ROI centers;
- requested spatial scales;
- confidence or an exploration score.

At time $t$, the causal sequence is:

1. observe $x_t^{LR}$ and the history of earlier crops;
2. predict $G_{t+\Delta}$;
3. acquire the selected high-resolution region;
4. give global and ROI tokens to the action model;
5. update memory and replan.

The selector cannot use the same current high-resolution crop it is deciding whether to
acquire. Full-resolution frames are valid during offline target construction, not as deployment
inputs.

### Representation for the downstream model

A native representation should expose the sensing structure:

```text
low-resolution global tokens
+ high-resolution ROI tokens
+ position and scale embeddings
+ explicit validity or mask metadata
```

A blurred-periphery image resized back to the model's expected dimensions is useful for
compatibility studies. It does not reduce token computation unless the downstream encoder also
changes.

### Selector objective

A general selector can combine:

$$
\mathcal L_g
=
\lambda_p\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}
+\lambda_u\mathcal L_{\mathrm{utility}}
+\lambda_h\mathcal L_{\mathrm{human}}
+\lambda_b\mathcal L_{\mathrm{budget}}
+\lambda_t\mathcal L_{\mathrm{temporal}}
+\lambda_d\mathcal L_{\mathrm{diversity}}.
$$

- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}$ distills teacher relevance.
- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{utility}}$ preserves actions or downstream task behavior.
- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{human}}$ optionally uses human gaze as an auxiliary prior.
- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{budget}}$ enforces a fixed high-resolution allocation.
- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{temporal}}$ represents sensor latency and discourages unnecessary jumps.
- $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{diversity}}$ keeps multiple ROIs from collapsing to one point.

For discrete hard selection, possible estimators include Gumbel top-$K$, straight-through
gradients, offline enumeration, or policy gradients. The spatial grid is merely a sensor-action
space; it does not encode object identity.

## Why the selector and action model should not begin from scratch together

Fully joint optimization permits arbitrary co-adaptation. A selector can choose an initially
meaningless region while the student learns to decode a spurious cue from that region. The pair
may work together but fail to reveal generally useful visual allocation.

A safer curriculum is:

1. train or reuse a competent full-resolution teacher;
2. train the foveated student with randomized masks so it can consume varied allocations;
3. distill validated teacher relevance into the selector;
4. freeze or mostly freeze the student while optimizing the selector for utility;
5. jointly fine-tune selector and student at a small learning rate;
6. evaluate with the same frozen selector distribution used during training.

Matched-source evaluation is essential. Swapping a new foveation pattern into a policy trained
on a different pattern confounds selector quality with visual distribution shift.

## Mode A and Mode B are different scientific claims

### Mode A: post-acquisition allocation

```text
current full-resolution image
→ choose a fovea
→ reduce downstream visual computation
```

Mode A can establish that resolution placement affects a model and may reduce downstream
compute. It cannot claim reduced camera readout or sensor bandwidth because the selector already
saw the full-resolution image.

### Mode B: acquisition-time foveation

```text
current low-resolution global view + past high-resolution crops
→ choose the next ROI
→ acquire only that high-resolution region
```

Only Mode B supports an acquisition-bandwidth claim. The existing experiments are Mode A; the
causal selector above is intended to cross into Mode B.

If a tiny target cannot be detected in the global stream, no one-step Mode-B selector can
recover it reliably. The principled responses are recurrent belief, exploratory scanning,
multiple ROIs, or a somewhat richer global stream—not hidden access to the current
full-resolution frame.

## The staged experimental program

### Gate 0 — decompose the current mechanism

Before making generalist claims:

- separate multiresolution token placement from the numeric gaze-coordinate side channel;
- compare identical predictor architectures trained on task gaze versus generic-saliency
  supervision.

This determines what the present task-specific advantage actually measures.

### Gate 1 — audit generalist relevance

On a common observation panel, extract:

- raw attention;
- attention rollout;
- gradient relevance;
- action-output gradients;
- patch deletion;
- resolution restoration.

Compare with task-specific predicted gaze, recorded human gaze, generic saliency, stationary
task priors, center, random, and shuffled maps.

**Advance criterion:** a proxy must beat simple priors and generic saliency on counterfactual
restoration utility—not merely correlate with human gaze.

### Gate 2 — measure causal foveation-value curves

At several visual budgets:

1. begin with the same low-resolution frame;
2. restore the top-ranked patch or top-$K$ patches;
3. measure full-resolution teacher-action fidelity;
4. estimate incremental value per high-resolution token;
5. identify thresholds and plateaus.

Report curves by task and phase. A selector may matter most near grasping, insertion, contact,
or occlusion even if episode-average fidelity changes only modestly.

### Gate 3 — distill a generalist selector

Train from low-resolution context, instruction, state, and short history. Use task-level—not
frame-level—held-out splits.

Evaluate:

- zero-shot relevance on unseen tasks;
- causal action-fidelity curves;
- selector memory and latency;
- behavior when current high-resolution input is explicitly absent.

### Gate 4 — few-shot task adaptation

Fine-tune the generalist selector with 1%, 5%, 10%, and 100% of:

- task-domain human gaze;
- task-domain causal-utility labels;
- or a hybrid.

Compare it with a task-specific selector trained from scratch on the same quantity of data. The
question is whether generalist pretraining reduces task-specific supervision.

### Gate 5 — train lightweight matched-source policies

Carry only the strongest selectors into complete policy training:

- task-domain reference;
- zero-shot generalist selector;
- few-shot human-adapted selector;
- few-shot utility-adapted selector;
- generic saliency and geometric anchors.

Every policy must train and roll out with its own frozen selector. Measure exact closed-loop
success, failure stage, visual budget, selector-plus-policy latency, and robustness to visual
shifts.

### Gate 6 — test WAM feedback before integration

Using the strongest standalone selector, compare:

| Variant | Additional selector information |
|---|---|
| Independent | Low-resolution view, instruction, state, history |
| Action feedback | Action hidden state, entropy, or ensemble disagreement |
| Video feedback | Predicted visual state or future uncertainty |
| Combined feedback | Both action and video signals |

Only causally available state can guide the next acquisition decision.

### Gate 7 — add a native gaze head or expert only if warranted

If feedback yields a reproducible benefit on predictive tasks, integrate foveation:

- attach a gaze head to shared states in a single-backbone model;
- use a dedicated foveation expert in an MoT only if specialization is useful;
- post-train video pathways on mixed-resolution observations before interpreting generated
  foveated futures;
- predict a receding-horizon sensing plan, execute one ROI, observe, and replan.

The standalone selector is not a temporary placeholder. It is the necessary baseline and may be
the best deployable system.

## A possible WAM-native objective

For video, action, and foveation outputs:

$$
\mathcal L
=
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{action}}
+\alpha\mathcal L_{\mathrm{world}}
+\beta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{gaze}}
+\gamma\mathcal L_{\mathrm{utility}}
+\delta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{budget}}
+\eta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{temporal}}.
$$

The gaze term can begin as teacher distillation and gradually shift toward downstream utility.
Training should include randomized or imperfect masks and the selector's own predictions to
reduce closed-loop drift.

A native MoT version might contain:

```text
video expert      → future state, motion, occlusion, uncertainty
action expert     → intended trajectory and action uncertainty
foveation expert  → ROI centers, scales, and scanpath
```

Shared attention can make sensing both action-conditioned and predictive. Strict attention masks
must enforce this chronology:

- before choosing $G_t$, the gaze stream can see current low-resolution and past
  high-resolution tokens;
- it cannot see unselected current high-resolution tokens;
- after acquisition, action and video streams can consume the selected current tokens;
- full current or future frames appear only in offline targets and teacher losses.

Without that contract, the model can appear to learn active sensing while quietly reading the
measurement it claims to avoid.

## Three evaluation axes that must remain separate

1. **Human-gaze agreement:** does a selector resemble human fixation?
2. **Causal information utility:** does its selected detail restore action-relevant information?
3. **Matched closed-loop control:** does a policy trained with that selector succeed?

Efficiency is a fourth axis:

4. **End-to-end cost:** does selector plus sensing plus student actually reduce latency,
   tokens, pixels, power, or bandwidth?

A method can perform well on one axis and poorly on another. Raw attention might resemble human
gaze but fail restoration. Synthetic utility might disagree with gaze and improve control. A
good selector might still be too slow to yield a net systems benefit.

## Main risks and the corresponding controls

| Risk | Consequence | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Raw attention is non-causal | Attractive heatmaps produce poor foveae | Require deletion, restoration, and rollout tests |
| Teacher-specific bias | The selector distills one model's quirks | Use multiple teachers or checkpoints and soft targets |
| Tiny target is absent at low resolution | The selector cannot localize it | Memory, exploration, multiple ROIs, richer global stream |
| Selector and student co-adapt | A brittle private code develops | Random-mask student training and staged freezing |
| Current full-resolution leakage | False active-sensing result | Causal input contracts and explicit no-HR ablations |
| One point is insufficient | Object-target interactions are lost | Top-$K$, scale allocation, and diversity loss |
| Attention measures importance, not resolution need | Already-visible objects dominate | Optimize low-to-high-resolution restoration value |
| Selector latency consumes the savings | No end-to-end benefit | Small selector, cached history, total latency accounting |
| Gaze acts as a numeric state hint | Visual allocation effect is confounded | Factor token placement and numeric conditioning |
| Mixed-resolution WAM generation is unstable | Future artifacts compound | Post-train the video path before autoregressive use |
| WAM integration adds complexity without value | Expensive negative result | Require a positive feedback ablation first |

## What would count as a useful outcome?

The program has several valid stopping points:

- If attention correlates with human gaze but fails causal restoration, attention is not a
  sufficient foveation target.
- If resolution-upgrade utility distills successfully, a small selector can amortize privileged
  action relevance from a full-resolution teacher.
- If a few task labels close most of the gap to a task-specific selector, generalist
  pretraining reduces gaze-data requirements.
- If the standalone selector matches a native expert, WAM-native foveation is unnecessary.
- If synthetic utility beats human gaze, robot-optimal sensing differs from human fixation.
- If human adaptation remains essential, task-specific gaze contains information not recovered
  by the generalist teacher.

The immediate high-value question is deliberately smaller than building a new WAM:

> Does an existing generalist action model contain a transferable spatial signal whose
> high-ranked regions causally recover more action information than generic saliency and simple
> priors?

Only after that answer is positive should the project spend the cost of training a native
foveation expert.
