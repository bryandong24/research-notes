World-action models are often grouped by names that mix together three different design
decisions:

1. **Diffusion or flow matching** specifies how a distribution over videos or actions is
   learned and sampled.
2. **A transformer** specifies a token-processing architecture. A **diffusion transformer
   (DiT)** is the more specific case in which a transformer is parameterized and conditioned
   to serve as a diffusion or flow denoiser.
3. **IDM, single-backbone, or Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT)** specifies how world prediction
   and action generation are connected.

These axes are largely independent. An inverse-dynamics model, a unified backbone, and an MoT
can all contain generic transformers, and any of their generative stages can use a DiT.
Conversely, a WAM family is not defined by whether its action sampler uses diffusion, flow
matching, autoregression, or a deterministic head.

That distinction is useful for foveation. It tells us whether mixed-resolution information is
entering a world predictor, a shared video-action representation, an action expert, or a
separate sensing policy.

## A compact diffusion and flow-matching primer

Suppose the desired output is $y$. It could be a future video latent, an action trajectory, or a
joint video-action state. In a simple [Flow Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747)
construction, training interpolates between noise $\epsilon$ and data $y$:

$$
y_\tau=(1-\tau)\epsilon+\tau y,
\qquad
v^*=y-\epsilon.
$$

A neural network learns the corresponding velocity field under context $c$:

$$
v_\theta(y_\tau,\tau,c)\approx v^*.
$$

At inference, the sampler begins at noise and integrates this field toward a clean sample.
Diffusion formulations differ in the path and in whether the network predicts noise, score,
velocity, or a clean target, but the architectural point is the same:

> When a [DiT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748) is used, its output at one sampling step is
> normally a denoising prediction, not the final image or robot command. A generic transformer
> elsewhere in the WAM may instead produce context features or autoregressive tokens.

After sampling:

- a clean **video latent** is converted to pixels by a visual decoder, commonly a VAE;
- a clean **action latent** is projected or unnormalized into joint positions, Cartesian
  deltas, gripper commands, or another robot action space.

The word *decoder* is therefore overloaded. It may refer to the diffusion network, a small
output projection, or the VAE that turns visual latents into pixels. Those are different
operations.

## How language enters a video/action diffusion model

A text-conditioned WAM does not usually learn a mapping from raw characters to video latents
with one arbitrary MLP. A typical path is:

```text
instruction
→ tokenizer
→ pretrained text encoder
→ text-token embeddings
→ projection to the WAM's hidden width
→ cross-attention, token concatenation, or adaptive normalization
```

The text encoder might be CLIP, T5, or the language pathway of a vision-language model.
Proprioception and past actions are embedded similarly. These conditioning mechanisms are
orthogonal to the WAM family: IDM, single-backbone, and MoT systems can all use them.

Let the available context be

$$
c_t=(o_{\leq t},q_{\leq t},l,a_{<t}),
$$

where $o$ is visual history, $q$ is robot state, $l$ is language, and $a$ is action history.
The architectural families differ mainly in what happens next.

## 1. Inverse-dynamics-style WAMs

An inverse-dynamics-style system separates a visual prediction problem from an action inference
problem:

$$
\hat z^v_{t+1:t+H}
=
W_\psi(o_{\leq t},l),
$$

$$
\hat a_{t:t+K}
\sim
D_\theta(o_{\leq t},q_t,l,\hat z^v_{t+1:t+H}).
$$

$W_\psi$ produces a visual plan or predictive representation. $D_\theta$ asks which action
sequence would connect the present state to that future. The inverse-dynamics stage can be a
diffusion or flow-matching action model, but the family is defined by this decomposition, not by
the sampler.

The predicted future also need not cross the model boundary as decoded RGB. The action model can
consume:

- generated future frames;
- future VAE latents;
- spatial tokens from the video model; or
- intermediate predictive features.

[Video Prediction Policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14803) is a useful representative. It
fine-tunes a text-guided video predictor for manipulation, then conditions a diffusion action
model on representations from that predictor.

### What an IDM is not

If a model takes a proposed action and predicts its consequence,

$$
\hat o_{t+1}=W(o_t,a_t),
$$

it is a forward dynamics model. A planner can evaluate many such candidate actions, but that is
not the same computation as first specifying a visual future and then inferring the actions
that reach it. Both approaches use world models; only the latter has the characteristic
inverse-dynamics split.

### Does the IDM also receive the video model's KV cache?

Not normally. A KV cache stores attention keys and values from earlier tokens so an
autoregressive transformer does not recompute them. It is primarily an execution optimization,
not a standardized representation of the predicted future.

Independent video and action models usually exchange frames, latents, or features—not an
opaque cache. A system could explicitly train an action model to consume another transformer's
cached state, but that is an architectural choice rather than part of the IDM definition.

### Foveation in an IDM pipeline

The simplest path keeps the selector external:

```text
low-resolution global view + selected high-resolution crops
→ visual predictor
→ future visual plan or latent features
→ inverse-dynamics action model
```

The predicted future can also help choose the *next* fovea: likely contacts, future object
positions, and uncertain occlusion boundaries are candidate sensing targets.

A more invasive design makes the predicted future itself mixed-resolution. That requires the
video model to generate coherent mixed-resolution latents and the IDM to train on the same
representation. Replacing a familiar uniform future with an unfamiliar mixed-resolution one
at evaluation time would mostly measure distribution shift.

The benefit of the IDM family is modularity. The cost is that selector error, world-model error,
and inverse-dynamics error can compound across interfaces.

## 2. Single-backbone WAMs

“Single backbone” means that video and action generation share substantial computation. It does
not require a single undifferentiated output tensor, nor does it imply that video and action
have identical decoders.

Two related designs are useful to distinguish.

### Design A: one joint denoiser

Noisy video and action variables are embedded into a common hidden width and processed by one
multimodal denoising transformer, often a DiT:

$$
h
=
T_\theta\left(
[E_v(z^v_\tau),E_a(a_\tau),E_c(c_t)],
\tau
\right).
$$

The video-token and action-token subsets are projected back to their own spaces:

$$
\hat v_v=P_v(h_v),
\qquad
\hat v_a=P_a(h_a).
$$

Here $P_v$ and $P_a$ can be lightweight linear or MLP projections. There may be no second large
transformer “decoder” after $T_\theta$. But the projections are still predicting the next
denoising direction. The sampler must integrate those predictions, and the final video latent
still requires a VAE decoder before it becomes pixels.

The model is unified because the same denoising transformer jointly reasons over video, action,
text, and state tokens.

### Design B: shared contextual trunk, separate diffusion heads

A second design shares the contextual representation while retaining substantial
modality-specific denoisers:

$$
h_{\mathrm{joint}}
=
T_\theta(o_{\leq t},q_{\leq t},l,\text{masked future tokens}),
$$

$$
\hat v_v
=
g_v(z^v_\tau,\tau,h_{\mathrm{joint}}),
\qquad
\hat v_a
=
g_a(a_\tau,\tau,h_{\mathrm{joint}}).
$$

The central correction is:

> $h_{\mathrm{joint}}$ is generally conditioning context, not a cleaned video/action latent.

$g_v$ and $g_a$ remain the generative heads that perform modality-specific denoising. This
allows video and action to use different tensor shapes, objectives, sampling schedules, and
output parameterizations while benefiting from a shared world/action representation.

[Unified Video Action Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00200) is a useful example of this
pattern. Its masked-autoregressive transformer constructs shared context, while dedicated
diffusion heads produce visual latents and actions.

| Question | Joint denoiser | Shared trunk + heads |
|---|---|---|
| Where does most denoising computation occur? | One multimodal transformer | Modality-specific heads conditioned by a shared trunk |
| What is the shared output? | Hidden states projected to video/action vector fields | Context used by downstream denoisers |
| Is a small linear output projection enough? | Often, before sampling and the visual VAE | Usually not; each head performs substantial generative work |
| Can schedules differ by modality? | Possible, but tightly coupled | Natural |
| Where does specialization live? | Input/output projections and token identity | Dedicated video/action heads |

These are endpoints rather than rigid boxes. Practical systems often share some layers and
specialize others.

### Foveation in a single backbone

A native mixed-resolution input can be represented as:

```text
low-resolution global tokens
+ high-resolution ROI tokens
+ location and scale embeddings
+ validity or mask metadata
```

This is preferable to making the model infer which pixels came from which resolution. A
same-shaped blurred-periphery raster remains a useful compatibility test, but it does not by
itself reduce transformer token count.

Because video and action already share hidden states, a small gaze head can predict the next
ROI from those states. A full third expert may be unnecessary.

Generation-time foveation is a separate problem. If the video head must generate a
mixed-resolution future, it needs mixed-resolution training or post-training. A video generator
trained only on uniform token layouts should not be expected to remain coherent after its
latent geometry is changed.

## 3. Mixture-of-Transformers WAMs

A Mixture-of-Transformers retains modality-specific parameterized streams while allowing their
tokens to interact:

```text
video tokens  → video expert ┐
                              ├→ shared or mixed attention
action tokens → action expert┘
```

The streams can have separate embeddings, normalizations, feed-forward layers, time
conditioning, and output projections. At corresponding layers, shared or mixed attention lets
the action expert read current or predicted visual state and lets the video expert incorporate
action information.

This use of *mixture* is not necessarily the sparse router found in a conventional
mixture-of-experts language model. It often means that modalities retain distinct transformer
paths but exchange information layer by layer.

[Fast-WAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16666) is a concrete example: a video DiT expert and
ActionDiT expert are coupled through mixed attention. Video and action objectives are learned
together, but action-only inference can reuse visual context without iteratively generating a
future video. This separates the benefit of predictive video co-training from the cost of
explicit imagination at every control step.

[LingBot-VA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21998) presents autoregressive video/action modeling,
asynchronous execution, and KV-cache reuse. One implementation detail matters when comparing
systems: the currently public LingBot-VA models are described by the authors as
**shared-backbone releases**. The separated-expert architecture discussed in the paper should
not be treated as identical to every released checkpoint.

### Foveation in an MoT

There are three increasing levels of coupling:

1. **External selector.** It produces mixed-resolution observations before either expert runs.
2. **Feedback selector.** A separate selector additionally receives compact, causally available
   action/video state or uncertainty from the preceding control step.
3. **Native foveation expert.**

```text
video expert      → predicts future visual state
action expert     → predicts an action trajectory
foveation expert  → predicts the next sensing decision
```

The third option is architecturally natural because sensing, video, and action already have
separate roles and a communication interface. It is also the easiest to make causally invalid.
The gaze expert cannot inspect an unselected current high-resolution token before deciding to
acquire it.

## The three families side by side

| Family | Core computation | Typical action path | Natural first foveation interface |
|---|---|---|---|
| IDM | Visual prediction followed by inverse dynamics | Predicted future features condition a separate action model | External selector; use predicted future to propose contacts |
| Single backbone | Shared video/action representation with joint or specialized output heads | Shared hidden states condition action denoising | Mixed-resolution tokens plus a gaze head on shared state |
| MoT | Video and action experts coupled by shared/mixed attention | Action expert reads current or predicted visual state | External or feedback selector; later a dedicated gaze expert |
| Spatial latent WAM | Predictive latent state without requiring RGB generation | Action reads spatially grounded latent state | Latent uncertainty or affordance maps, when spatial grounding exists |

The families are a continuum. A model should be classified by its actual computational graph,
not by whether its title contains “world model,” nor by a conceptual diagram that differs from
the released implementation.

## Input foveation and output foveation are different claims

Foveation can occur at several boundaries:

1. **Sensor allocation:** decide which high-resolution measurements to acquire.
2. **Input computation:** encode a low-resolution global view and a few high-resolution ROIs.
3. **Action-conditioned relevance:** use a full-resolution teacher to discover which spatial
   detail affects actions.
4. **Future generation:** predict only selected future regions at high resolution.

The first two directly target perception and control cost. The fourth also changes the
generative model's output distribution and is substantially riskier.

[Foveated Diffusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23491) provides the relevant warning:
mixed-resolution token layouts require appropriate post-training; a naive pretrained
diffusion transformer can produce structural and scale inconsistencies. The same concern
applies to an autoregressive video WAM, where artifacts may be fed back as future context.

For that reason, the near-term research path is deliberately conservative:

```text
validate useful fovea placement
→ train a model to consume varied mixed-resolution inputs
→ learn a causal standalone selector
→ test whether WAM state improves that selector
→ modify future-video generation only if the earlier gates succeed
```

## A practical terminology checklist

- **Backbone:** the major shared feature-processing network.
- **Expert:** a modality-specific parameterized transformer stream.
- **Diffusion/flow head:** predicts the denoising vector for one output space.
- **Output projection:** maps hidden width to video-latent or action dimensions.
- **VAE decoder:** maps a sampled visual latent to pixels.
- **KV cache:** stored attention state used to avoid recomputing history.
- **Foveation selector:** chooses spatial resolution allocation; it is not automatically part of
  the action model.

Keeping these objects separate makes the design question precise. The central issue is not
whether a WAM “has a decoder.” It is which representation carries task-relevant visual detail,
which computation generates the action, and whether the sensing decision is made before or
after high-resolution information is available.
