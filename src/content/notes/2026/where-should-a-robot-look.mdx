Foveated perception offers a simple bargain: preserve a small region at high resolution and
represent the periphery more cheaply. The unresolved question is not whether we can build a
mixed-resolution image or tokenizer. It is **where the high-resolution region should go**.

We tested that question on GIAVA's simulated thread-needle task. Each visual condition trained
its own complete downstream action policy, and the same gaze source was used during training
and rollout. This removes a major confound from an earlier source-swap experiment: a model no
longer fails merely because it encounters an unfamiliar foveation pattern at test time.

This one rollout is illustrative, not the estimate of performance. It was the first fixed
evaluation seed, selected by protocol rather than by outcome. In it, both task-specific gaze and
DeepGaze complete the task while the low-resolution policy stops at stage 3. The aggregate experiment is
what separates them reliably: the task-specific predictor succeeds more often across the paired
evaluation set.

Three behaviors are worth noticing:

1. **The task predictor tracks external task geometry.** It tends to remain near the thread,
   needle, target, or contact-relevant workspace.
2. **Generic saliency often spends detail on the robot itself.** The hands are large, moving,
   and visually salient, but they are not always where another increment of spatial resolution
   changes the next action.
3. **A good spatial prior is useful but incomplete.** A fixed point learned from the human-gaze
   distribution reaches 71.3%—a higher point estimate than image center—but it cannot adapt as
   the relevant geometry changes through the episode.

## A placement sanity check in PushT

An earlier frozen-policy assay asked a deliberately narrower question: if we preserve the same
amount of source-image detail, does *where* we place it affect a released policy? At a fixed
6.25% simulated source-pixel budget, object-centered foveation succeeded on 31 of 50 PushT
rollouts (62%), compared with 10 of 50 for uniform low resolution (20%) and 4 of 50 for a fixed
random location (8%).

This is a **compatibility and placement assay**, not a speed result. The released UVA policy was
not retrained for each visual condition, and the experiment simulated foveation in a
same-shaped raster. Its role in the evidence chain is simply to show that useful detail has
strong spatial structure before we pay the cost of matched policy training.

## What changes in the research plan

The strongest result is not that human-like gaze is universally optimal. It is that
**task-conditioned allocation beats generic visual saliency under matched downstream
training**. Human gaze is currently our strongest supervision source, but it may not be the
best possible target for a robot.

The next question is therefore whether a generalist VLA or world-action model already contains
a transferable proxy for useful gaze. Its action-conditioned attention, gradients, or
counterfactual patch sensitivity can be extracted offline. Those signals do not save compute
by themselves; they supervise a much smaller model that must predict high-resolution locations
from low-resolution context at deployment.

<PipelineDiagram />

The target is not simply “what is visually important?” A region may be important yet already
recognizable in the low-resolution stream. What we want is the **marginal action value of
additional spatial detail**:

> If this patch is upgraded from low to high resolution, how much action-relevant information
> is recovered?

The immediate sequence of experiments is:

1. Compare action-conditioned attention, gradient relevance, and low-to-high-resolution
   restoration value from an existing VLA/WAM against task-specific gaze.
2. Test each proxy causally: preserve its top-ranked patches under the same budget and measure
   action fidelity, not just heatmap correlation.
3. Distill the best proxy into a small, causal gaze policy that sees low-resolution context,
   language, proprioception, and history.
4. Train a lightweight downstream VLA/WAM on the exact distribution produced by that selector.
5. Add action- or video-state feedback only if the independent selector leaves a measurable
   gap; a native foveation expert is a later hypothesis, not a starting assumption.

## Interpretation boundaries

- **This is post-acquisition foveation.** The gaze source can inspect the current
  full-resolution simulator image. The study demonstrates allocation of downstream visual
  computation, not reduced camera readout or sensor bandwidth.
- **The blurred raster is illustrative.** Policy actions were computed through GIAVA's actual
  mixed-resolution tokenization path. The displayed 124-pixel clear square is centered at the
  true tokenizer gaze, but the policy did not act from the rendered blur.
- **Gaze enters by two paths.** It controls token placement and is also appended numerically to
  proprioceptive conditioning. A causal factorial is still needed to isolate those effects.
- **This is one precision task in simulation.** The result motivates generalist and
  cross-task tests; it does not establish universal robot-optimal gaze.
- **Condition A predicts human-like gaze; it is not live human gaze.** Recorded eye tracking
  supervises the released task-domain U-Net, which runs autonomously during rollout.
- **A and DeepGaze are not identical predictor architectures.** They also differ in training
  data and preprocessing, so the current result ranks complete gaze-source systems rather than
  isolating supervision labels alone.
- **Full and low resolution are contextual anchors.** They use different released MAE
  initializations and tokenizers, so their difference is not a clean resolution-only ablation.
- **A single video is not a rate.** The paired multi-seed evaluation, rather than the featured
  example, supports the quantitative claim.

## Technical appendix

<details>
  <summary>Experimental protocol</summary>

  Seven visual conditions were evaluated: task-specific GIAVA U-Net gaze, image center, a
  stationary task-specific gaze prior, DeepGaze IIE with MIT prior, DeepGaze IIE with a uniform
  prior, uniform full resolution, and uniform low resolution.

  For each condition, three complete policies were trained with policy seeds 42, 43, and 44.
  Foveated policies used source-consistent gaze during both training and rollout. The
  foveated ViT, projection layers, attention pool, proprioceptive projection, and flow-matching
  action DiT were trained for 30,000 updates. The gaze source remained frozen.

  Every final EMA checkpoint was evaluated on the same 50 IID environment seeds, 2000–2049,
  with a fixed flow-sampling RNG. The primary outcome was exact thread-needle completion
  (reward stage 5). Aggregates use a paired hierarchical bootstrap over policy seed and
  environment seed.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>All IID conditions</summary>

  | Visual condition | Exact success |
  |---|---:|
  | Task-specific GIAVA U-Net | **86.0%** |
  | Stationary task-specific prior | 71.3% |
  | DeepGaze IIE + MIT prior | 70.0% |
  | Uniform full resolution | 64.7% |
  | Fixed center | 63.3% |
  | DeepGaze IIE + uniform prior | 53.3% |
  | Uniform low resolution | 53.3% |

  Mean valid-token counts differed by only 1.507% across the five foveated conditions, below
  the predeclared threshold for an equalized-token control.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Robustness and negative results</summary>

  Under a distractor intervention that added dynamic, collidable tabletop objects,
  task-specific gaze retained 68.0% exact success; the full- and low-resolution anchors
  reached 44.0% and 26.0%.

  All tested conditions fell to 0% exact success under the current lighting suite. That
  intervention replaces neutral directional illumination with a randomized RGB spotlight,
  so it is a severe renderer shift rather than evidence about ordinary lighting variation.
  The failure is retained as a canonical negative result and requires a component-level
  diagnostic before broader interpretation.
</details>
